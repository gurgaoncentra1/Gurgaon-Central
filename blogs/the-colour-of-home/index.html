The Colour of Home

By Miriam

I went in for the Wi-Fi. I stayed because of a seven-year-old with a croissant.

It was a Saturday, and I had claimed the corner table, like usual, with the confidence of someone who had a deadline and no plan. Two tables away, a mother and her daughter sat down with the kind of seriousness usually reserved for board meetings. The girl had a small notebook. The mother had her phone face down on the table.

“Okay,” the girl said. “Today I’m the interviewer.”

“And I’m?”

“The person being interviewed. Obviously.”

I pretended to type. I did not type.

The first question was, “What is your favourite colour and why?” The mother said yellow, because it reminded her of the kitchen in her old house. The girl wrote this down very slowly, tongue between her teeth, and then asked, “Why did you leave the house?” The mother paused, then said, “We moved for work, beta. Papa’s job, and then mine.” The girl nodded like a tiny journalist who had heard this excuse before. “Do you miss it?” “Sometimes. Mostly the yellow kitchen.”

The questions got better. “What are you scared of?” The mother said, “Being late for you.” The girl stopped writing. She looked up, then reached across and patted her mother’s hand twice, the way adults pat children, and said, “That’s okay. I’m patient.” The mother laughed so suddenly she had to cover her mouth. So did I, into my laptop, which was unfortunate for the person on my video call who had just asked me something about quarterly numbers.

The interview ended, as all good interviews do, with a snack negotiation. The girl wanted a second croissant. The mother said one was enough. The girl said, “Journalists need energy.” The mother, clearly defeated by excellent logic, went to the counter.

While she was gone, the girl turned to me. Children always notice when you’re watching. “Are you working?” she asked.

“Trying to,” I said.

“You should take a break. My mom says people who stare at screens forget to blink.”

I blinked, deliberately, and she looked satisfied.

When they left, the girl waved with her whole arm, and her mother mouthed a sorry that I waved off. The table was left with crumbs and one torn page from the notebook. I didn’t read it.

I closed my laptop. The deadline could wait for my evening shift.

Outside, I stood by the traffic and did something I hadn’t done in months. I called my mother in Bangalore, at an hour when I’d usually text.

“Is something wrong?” she said, the way she always does when I call without a reason.

“No. I just wanted to ask you something. What’s your favourite colour?”

A long pause. The kind where I could hear the television in the background being muted.

“Why are you asking me this?”

“It’s an interview.”

She laughed, and then she went quiet, and then she said, “Pink. The pink flowers that used to fall in front of our house in Bangalore. I used to sweep them up every morning and wish they’d stay. Why did I never tell you that?”

“I don’t know,” I said. “Tell me now.”

So she did, and I walked all the way home without noticing the traffic.
